\chapter{Critical Analysis of Agile Modeling and Analytical Integration}

\section*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Introduction}

Chapter~1 established that scalability in decision support architectures has two independent dimensions: structural scalability, the capacity to absorb source change without re-engineering; and analytical scalability, the capacity to reach progressively higher levels of the decision support maturity spectrum. It also established that no surveyed paradigm satisfies both simultaneously. That finding is the entry point for this chapter.

This chapter moves from survey to critical analysis. It examines the two candidate components of a dual-scalable architecture — Data Vault~2.0 on the modeling side, and the Lakehouse's native analytics stack on the analytical side — not as independent topics but as elements that must eventually compose. The four sections converge toward a single question: do they actually compose, and if so, under what conditions and with what trade-offs?


\section{Data Vault 2.0 and the Claim to Structural Agility}
\label{sec:ch2-dv2}

Data Vault~2.0 is the modeling discipline this thesis nominates for the structural axis of a dual-scalable DSIS \cite{linstedt2015}. Its central claim is that structural change can be made additive rather than destructive. This section examines the mechanics behind that claim, scores them against the C1 structural dimensions defined in Section~\ref{sec:ch1-scalability}, and states the drawbacks honestly, because those drawbacks set up the composability tension developed later in the chapter.

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{Hub} — Stores only the business key: the stable, source-independent identifier the organization uses to recognize an entity (a customer, an order, a product). A Hub holds no attributes and no relationships. Once created, it never changes; absorbing a new source entity means adding a new Hub alongside the existing ones.
  \item \textbf{Link} — Stores the relationship between two or more Hubs, and nothing else. Relationships are tracked independently of the entities they connect. A new business relationship becomes a new Link; existing Hubs and Satellites remain untouched.
  \item \textbf{Satellite} — Stores the descriptive, frequently changing attributes of a Hub or a Link. Each row carries a load timestamp; new versions are appended rather than overwritten, so the full attribute history accumulates as a natural consequence of the loading pattern.
\end{itemize}

\medskip

Two further layers govern data governance \cite{varga2014}:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{Raw Vault} — Captures source data exactly as received, applying no business logic. It is the auditable, unmodified source-of-record.
  \item \textbf{Business Vault} — Holds derived constructs — computed satellites, bridge tables, interpreted business rules — isolated from the raw layer so that rule changes never rewrite source history.
\end{itemize}

Consumers do not query either vault layer directly. A downstream dimensional mart reshapes the vault into the star schemas that reporting tools expect \cite{kimball2013}.

\subsection{Why This Topology Yields Structural Agility}
\label{subsec:ch2-agility}

The agility of Data Vault~2.0 follows directly from the decomposition: because keys, relationships, and context are stored separately, structural change is additive. Absorbing a new source means adding hubs, links, and satellites alongside the existing ones, not altering them.

Loading is insert-only. The model never updates or deletes a row; it appends a new version with a fresh timestamp \cite{linstedt2015}. Full history is therefore a by-product of the loading pattern rather than a feature bolted on through slowly-changing-dimension logic.

Insert-only loading also confines the blast radius of change. A structural change in one source touches only the objects attached to that source, leaving the rest of the model and its downstream consumers untouched. The cost of change scales with the change itself, not with the size of the model.

\subsection{Operating Principles}
\label{subsec:ch2-principles}

Four operating principles turn the topology into a loadable, scalable system.

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{Hash-key surrogates} — Business keys are hashed into fixed-length surrogate keys so that loads can compute keys independently and in parallel, without lookups against previously loaded data \cite{linstedt2015}. This decouples the loading of each object and lets ingestion scale horizontally.
  \item \textbf{Insert-only loading} — Loads only append. The absence of updates and deletes removes lock contention, makes loads idempotent and restartable, and yields a complete audit trail by construction \cite{vassiliadis2009}.
  \item \textbf{Raw Vault / Business Vault separation} — The raw layer stays free of business logic so it can serve as the auditable source-of-record; derived logic is quarantined in the Business Vault, so a change to a business rule never rewrites raw history \cite{varga2014}.
  \item \textbf{Downstream consumption mart} — The vault is optimized for loading and historization, not for querying. A dimensional mart materializes the user-facing layer, accepting query-time cost in exchange for load-time agility \cite{kimball2013}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Documented Drawbacks}
\label{subsec:ch2-drawbacks}

The same separation that buys structural agility imposes design and implementation complexity. Modeling an entity across hubs, links, and satellites multiplies the number of objects, and getting the decomposition right demands skill that is scarce in practice. Adoption surveys report the skills requirement (48\%) and implementation complexity (35\%) as the leading barriers \cite{analyticscreator2025} \textit{(industry source; not peer-reviewed)}.

Query performance is the second cost. Reconstructing a single business view means joining a hub to its links and satellites, so analytical queries become join-heavy and slow on the vault itself; the same surveys flag query performance (32\%) as a recurring complaint \cite{analyticscreator2025}. The downstream mart exists precisely to absorb this cost, which is why vault data is never user-ready on its own.

These drawbacks are not incidental. The join-heavy query profile and the mandatory consumption layer become structurally important when Data Vault~2.0 is composed with a file-based Lakehouse, whose performance characteristics differ sharply from the relational engines the model assumes. That composability question is examined in Section~\ref{sec:ch2-tension}.

The standard is also a living one. Recent enhancements relax some of these costs: a virtualized load-end-date removes a maintenance-heavy update from satellite loading, and hybrid architectures push staging and hash-key computation onto a data lake before populating the vault \cite{giebler2019dv}. These moves already point toward the Lakehouse, and they motivate the analytical and compositional analysis that follows.

\section{Analytical Integration Patterns}
\label{sec:ch2-analytics}

Section~\ref{sec:ch2-dv2} assessed Data Vault~2.0 on the structural axis. This section turns to the analytical axis and examines how a modern Lakehouse pipeline integrates predictive capability \cite{armbrust2021}. Three patterns define that integration: the order in which data is transformed, the way models are embedded in the pipeline, and the mechanisms that keep data fresh. Each is mapped onto the C1 analytical-scalability dimensions, and the limits are kept in view for the composability analysis that follows.

\subsection{From ETL to ELT}
\label{subsec:ch2-elt}

The first pattern is a reordering of the pipeline. The classical warehouse transforms data \emph{before} loading it (ETL), because storage was expensive and the target schema was fixed in advance \cite{vassiliadis2009}. The Lakehouse inverts this: it loads raw data first and transforms it \emph{in place} (ELT), because object storage is cheap and compute can be pushed down to the data \cite{harby2024}.

ELT is what makes the medallion layering of Chapter~1 possible: raw data lands in Bronze, is refined into Silver, and is served from Gold, all within one governed store \cite{databricks2022} \textit{(industry practice; not peer-reviewed)}. Transformation becomes incremental rather than a precondition for ingestion.

\subsection{Native Predictive Integration}
\label{subsec:ch2-native-ml}

The second pattern concerns where predictive models live. The decisive question for analytical scalability is whether models are first-class citizens of the pipeline or external appendages \cite{armbrust2021}.

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{Downstream bolt-on} — Data is exported to a separate machine-learning system. Governance and lineage are lost at the boundary, and the copy used for training drifts from the data used for reporting.
  \item \textbf{Native integration} — Training, serving, and monitoring run on the same governed store. One copy of the data feeds both business intelligence and machine learning, and lineage is preserved end to end.
\end{itemize}

Native integration assembles the model lifecycle into a closed loop on a single platform \cite{kreuzberger2023}. Figure~\ref{fig:native-ml-loop} shows the arrangement.

The loop has four working parts \cite{kreuzberger2023}:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{Feature store} — Curates and serves features consistently to both training and inference, removing training–serving skew.
  \item \textbf{Model training} — Runs on the same governed data, so models train on the trusted production record rather than a stale export.
  \item \textbf{Serving / inference} — Scores new data and writes predictions back to the store, keeping results inside the governed lineage.
  \item \textbf{Feedback and retraining} — Monitoring detects drift and triggers retraining, closing the loop required for prescriptive analytics \cite{feedback2025}.
\end{itemize}

This native arrangement is what lets a Lakehouse reach the predictive and prescriptive levels of the maturity spectrum without a downstream bolt-on \cite{futurei2025}.

\subsection{Streaming and Incremental Analytics}
\label{subsec:ch2-streaming}

The third pattern keeps the pipeline fresh. Prescriptive decisions need data recent enough to act on, which shifts ingestion and scoring from periodic batches toward continuous flow.

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{Change data capture (CDC)} — Streams row-level changes from source systems as they happen, rather than reloading whole tables \cite{kleppmann2017}.
  \item \textbf{Continuous ingestion} — A single replayable stream feeds both real-time and historical processing, the Kappa pattern \cite{kreps2014} \textit{(industry practice; not peer-reviewed)}.
  \item \textbf{Near-real-time scoring} — Models score incoming records on arrival, so predictions track the current state of the business.
  \item \textbf{Online learning over drift} — Where distributions shift, models update incrementally on the stream rather than waiting for a full retrain \cite{bifet2018}.
\end{itemize}

\section{The Core Tension: Composing DV2.0 with the Lakehouse}
\label{sec:ch2-tension}

The previous two sections established a complementarity: Data Vault~2.0 is structurally strong, the Lakehouse analytics stack is analytically strong, and each covers the other's weak dimension. The obvious move is to combine them. This section asks whether they actually compose, or whether one is quietly bent to fit the other. The answer matters, because the whole thesis rests on the two forming a single coherent architecture rather than an uneasy stack.

\subsection{The Clash, Stated Precisely}
\label{subsec:ch2-clash}

The two paradigms come from different worlds. Data Vault~2.0 is a \emph{relational-lineage modeling discipline}: structured tables, enforced keys, explicit historization, and business views reconstructed through joins \cite{linstedt2015}. The Lakehouse is \emph{file-based and schema-flexible}: open columnar files, a tendency toward schema-on-read, and transactional guarantees layered on top of files rather than tables \cite{armbrust2021, deltalake2020}.

\subsection{What Industry Practice Shows}
\label{subsec:ch2-practice}

In practice, the industry has already chosen a direction: Data Vault~2.0 is implemented \emph{on top of} the Lakehouse storage layer, not as an alternative to it \cite{databricks2022} \textit{(industry practice; not peer-reviewed)}. The vault tables are stored as Delta tables, ingestion uses auto-loaders, and loading is orchestrated through declarative pipelines. Figure~\ref{fig:ch2-dv2-on-lakehouse} shows the canonical placement.

\fig[\textwidth]{dv2-on-lakehouse-databricks.png}{Data Vault~2.0 mapped onto the Lakehouse medallion. Source: \cite{databricks2022} \textit{(industry/vendor source; not peer-reviewed)}.}{ch2-dv2-on-lakehouse}

The mapping is direct. File staging feeds the Bronze landing zones; the Raw Vault and Business Vault occupy the Silver layer; star-schema marts and point-in-time views are served from Gold. The diagram makes the key fact visible: in current practice the vault is a \emph{tenant} of the Lakehouse, not a competitor to it.

Independent and vendor accounts converge on the same pattern. Hybrid designs push staging and hash-key computation onto the lake before populating the vault \cite{moermans2023, moronta2026} \textit{(industry practice; not peer-reviewed)}, and a peer-reviewed study reports the same approach for modeling data lakes with Data Vault \cite{giebler2019dv}.

The sharpest conflict is performance. The governance conflict is subtler and just as real. The vault imposes discipline; the lake removes friction from landing raw data. A combined architecture has to hold both at once, and nothing in either paradigm forces that balance — it has to be designed in.

\subsection{What the Literature Does Not Resolve}
\label{subsec:ch2-unresolved}

The decisive observation of this chapter is what is \emph{missing}. The composition is asserted and practiced, but not methodically evaluated.

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{No peer-reviewed evaluation} — The combination is documented almost entirely in vendor blogs and practitioner guides, not in peer-reviewed studies \cite{databricks2022, moermans2023, moronta2026}.
  \item \textbf{Undocumented trade-offs} — The performance and governance costs above are acknowledged informally but rarely measured or modeled in the academic literature.
  \item \textbf{Surveys stop short} — Recent peer-reviewed surveys of the Lakehouse map its capabilities but do not assess its composition with a formal modeling discipline like DV2.0 \cite{schneider2024, harby2024}.
\end{itemize}

This unresolved state is itself a finding. The industry has run ahead of the evidence, combining the two paradigms by convention rather than by demonstrated principle. That gap — a practiced combination without a reasoned framework — is the opening this thesis addresses. Section~\ref{sec:ch2-criteria} turns the alignments and conflicts above into the explicit criteria that Chapter~3 uses to build and assess that framework.

\section*{Conclusion}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Conclusion}

This chapter set the two candidate components against each other. Data Vault~2.0 is structurally agile but analytically capped and costly to query; the Lakehouse analytics stack is the mirror image, natively predictive but reliant on a data discipline it does not supply. Their strengths are complementary, and the industry already combines them, placing the vault on top of the Lakehouse, yet that combination rests on vendor practice rather than peer-reviewed evidence.

That gap is what construct~C2 is built to close. Its six rubric-backed criteria turn the alignments and conflicts of this chapter into an instrument, which Chapter~3 applies to design and evaluate a combined reference framework.
