\chapter{Critical Analysis of Agile Modeling and Analytical Integration}

\section*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Introduction}

Chapter~1 established that scalability in decision support architectures has two independent dimensions: structural scalability, the capacity to absorb source change without re-engineering; and analytical scalability, the capacity to reach progressively higher levels of the decision support maturity spectrum. It also established that no surveyed paradigm satisfies both simultaneously. That finding is the entry point for this chapter.

This chapter moves from survey to critical analysis. It examines the two candidate components of a dual-scalable architecture — Data Vault~2.0 on the modeling side, and the Lakehouse's native analytics stack on the analytical side — not as independent topics but as elements that must eventually compose. The four sections converge toward a single question: do they actually compose, and if so, under what conditions and with what trade-offs?


\section{Data Vault 2.0 and the Claim to Structural Agility}
\label{sec:ch2-dv2}

Data Vault~2.0 is the modeling discipline this thesis nominates for the structural axis of a dual-scalable DSIS \cite{linstedt2015}. Its central claim is that structural change can be made additive rather than destructive. This section examines the mechanics behind that claim, scores them against the C1 structural dimensions defined in Section~\ref{sec:ch1-scalability}, and states the drawbacks honestly, because those drawbacks set up the composability tension developed later in the chapter.

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{Hub} — Stores only the business key: the stable, source-independent identifier the organization uses to recognize an entity (a customer, an order, a product). A Hub holds no attributes and no relationships. Once created, it never changes; absorbing a new source entity means adding a new Hub alongside the existing ones.
  \item \textbf{Link} — Stores the relationship between two or more Hubs, and nothing else. Relationships are tracked independently of the entities they connect. A new business relationship becomes a new Link; existing Hubs and Satellites remain untouched.
  \item \textbf{Satellite} — Stores the descriptive, frequently changing attributes of a Hub or a Link. Each row carries a load timestamp; new versions are appended rather than overwritten, so the full attribute history accumulates as a natural consequence of the loading pattern.
\end{itemize}

\medskip

Two further layers govern data governance \cite{varga2014}:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{Raw Vault} — Captures source data exactly as received, applying no business logic. It is the auditable, unmodified source-of-record.
  \item \textbf{Business Vault} — Holds derived constructs — computed satellites, bridge tables, interpreted business rules — isolated from the raw layer so that rule changes never rewrite source history.
\end{itemize}

Consumers do not query either vault layer directly. A downstream dimensional mart reshapes the vault into the star schemas that reporting tools expect \cite{kimball2013}.

\subsection{Why This Topology Yields Structural Agility}
\label{subsec:ch2-agility}

The agility of Data Vault~2.0 follows directly from the decomposition: because keys, relationships, and context are stored separately, structural change is additive. Absorbing a new source means adding hubs, links, and satellites alongside the existing ones, not altering them.

Loading is insert-only. The model never updates or deletes a row; it appends a new version with a fresh timestamp \cite{linstedt2015}. Full history is therefore a by-product of the loading pattern rather than a feature bolted on through slowly-changing-dimension logic.

Insert-only loading also confines the blast radius of change. A structural change in one source touches only the objects attached to that source, leaving the rest of the model and its downstream consumers untouched. The cost of change scales with the change itself, not with the size of the model.

\subsection{Operating Principles}
\label{subsec:ch2-principles}

Four operating principles turn the topology into a loadable, scalable system.

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=4pt]
  \item[\textit{Hash-key surrogates}] Business keys are hashed into fixed-length surrogate keys so that loads can compute keys independently and in parallel, without lookups against previously loaded data \cite{linstedt2015}. This decouples the loading of each object and lets ingestion scale horizontally.
  \item[\textit{Insert-only loading}] Loads only append. The absence of updates and deletes removes lock contention, makes loads idempotent and restartable, and yields a complete audit trail by construction \cite{vassiliadis2009}.
  \item[\textit{Raw Vault / Business Vault separation}] The raw layer stays free of business logic so it can serve as the auditable source-of-record; derived logic is quarantined in the Business Vault, so a change to a business rule never rewrites raw history \cite{varga2014}.
  \item[\textit{Downstream consumption mart}] The vault is optimized for loading and historization, not for querying. A dimensional mart materializes the user-facing layer, accepting query-time cost in exchange for load-time agility \cite{kimball2013}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Documented Drawbacks}
\label{subsec:ch2-drawbacks}

The same separation that buys structural agility imposes design and implementation complexity. Modeling an entity across hubs, links, and satellites multiplies the number of objects, and getting the decomposition right demands skill that is scarce in practice. Adoption surveys report the skills requirement (48\%) and implementation complexity (35\%) as the leading barriers \todo{cite: BARC/Eckerson DV2.0 adoption survey}.

Query performance is the second cost. Reconstructing a single business view means joining a hub to its links and satellites, so analytical queries become join-heavy and slow on the vault itself; the same surveys flag query performance (32\%) as a recurring complaint \todo{cite: BARC/Eckerson DV2.0 adoption survey}. The downstream mart exists precisely to absorb this cost, which is why vault data is never user-ready on its own.

These drawbacks are not incidental. The join-heavy query profile and the mandatory consumption layer become structurally important when Data Vault~2.0 is composed with a file-based Lakehouse, whose performance characteristics differ sharply from the relational engines the model assumes. That composability question is examined in Section~\ref{sec:ch2-tension}.

The standard is also a living one. Recent enhancements relax some of these costs: a virtualized load-end-date removes a maintenance-heavy update from satellite loading, and hybrid architectures push staging and hash-key computation onto a data lake before populating the vault \cite{giebler2019dv}. These moves already point toward the Lakehouse, and they motivate the analytical and compositional analysis that follows.
