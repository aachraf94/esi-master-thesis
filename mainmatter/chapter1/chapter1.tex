\section*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Introduction}

This chapter builds the conceptual instrument the rest of the thesis depends on. The survey literature on decision support architectures is large, but it treats structural evolution and analytical capability as separate concerns. No single lens reads an architecture for both at once. This chapter supplies that lens.

\noindent The argument moves from vocabulary to instrument:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{\S\ref{sec:ch1-dsis}} positions the data warehouse as the operational core of DSIS and traces the move from reporting to modern analytical platforms.
  \item \textbf{\S\ref{sec:ch1-dw-paradigms}} examines the two classical modeling paradigms and the shared assumption that limits them.
  \item \textbf{\S\ref{sec:ch1-modern-arch}} surveys the modern architectures that arose to relax that assumption.
  \item \textbf{\S\ref{sec:ch1-maturity}} sets out the analytical maturity spectrum and the demand each level places on the architecture.
  \item \textbf{\S\ref{sec:ch1-scalability}} consolidates these observations into the dual-scalability conceptualization, the construct \textbf{C1}.
  \item \textbf{\S\ref{sec:ch1-gap}} re-reads every surveyed approach through that lens and isolates the research gap.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The survey sections are not neutral. Each one asks a single question about every paradigm it describes: \textit{does this property concern structural evolution or analytical capability?} That question is what turns a reading list into the instrument delivered in \S\ref{sec:ch1-scalability}.

\section{Decision Support Information Systems: Concepts and Evolution}
\label{sec:ch1-dsis}

Decision Support Information Systems (DSIS) are computer-based systems that supply managers with the data and analysis needed for non-routine decisions. Four terms recur throughout the thesis and are fixed here:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{DSS (Decision Support System)} the origin concept: a system that supports, rather than automates, managerial decisions.
  \item \textbf{BI (Business Intelligence)} the technologies and practices for gathering, integrating, and analyzing information to improve decision making \cite{watson2009}.
  \item \textbf{Data warehouse (DW)} the integrated, query-oriented store that feeds the analytical layer; the operational core of the DSIS.
  \item \textbf{DSIS (as used here)} the data-warehouse-centric decision support systems that descend from the DSS concept and its consolidation under BI.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The table makes one constant visible: every era kept the data warehouse as its core while widening the questions the system could answer \cite{chen2012, davenport2007}. A peer-reviewed survey traces the same arc at the storage layer, from data warehouse to data lake to Lakehouse \cite{nambiar2022}. Across all four eras runs a single assumption \textemdash{} that requirements can be specified before ingestion begins. That assumption is exactly what classical warehouse modeling encodes, and it is where the next section opens.

\section{Classical Data Warehouse Modeling Paradigms}
\label{sec:ch1-dw-paradigms}

Two modeling paradigms have governed data warehouse design for three decades. They differ sharply in approach yet share the limitation that motivates this thesis: both assume a stable, pre-specified schema. A third paradigm, Data Vault~2.0, was built to escape that limitation and is previewed at the end of this section.

\paragraph{Inmon \textemdash{} Corporate Information Factory (CIF).}
A normalized, enterprise-wide warehouse built top-down \cite{inmon2005}.

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{Core} a subject-oriented, integrated, non-volatile, time-variant repository in third normal form (3NF), from which dependent data marts are derived.
  \item \textbf{Strength} enterprise integration \textemdash{} one normalized model enforces consistency across the organization.
  \item \textbf{Weakness} long time-to-value and schema rigidity \textemdash{} the enterprise model must be largely complete before it delivers, and structural revision propagates through the normalized core.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Kimball \textemdash{} dimensional model.}
A warehouse built bottom-up from business processes \cite{kimball2013}.

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{Core} star and snowflake schemas of fact tables surrounded by conformed dimensions; slowly changing dimension (SCD) types~1, 2, and~3 record attribute history at different fidelities; the ETL discipline is codified separately \cite{kimball2004etl}.
  \item \textbf{Strength} query simplicity and fast delivery of business intelligence.
  \item \textbf{Weakness} schema changes are expensive and auditability is partial \textemdash{} SCD mechanisms record only the changes the designer anticipated.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The shared constraint.}
Read against a formal design perspective, the two paradigms diverge on method but converge on a constraint \cite{golfarelli2009}. Both assume a stable analytical perimeter and a schema fixed at design time. Neither was engineered for continuous schema evolution, and neither offers a native path toward predictive or prescriptive capability. The rigidity is built into the modeling discipline itself, not into any particular implementation.

\paragraph{Data Vault~2.0 (preview).}
The third major paradigm, designed specifically to absorb schema evolution and source change without re-engineering \cite{linstedt2015}. It decomposes the model into \textit{hubs}, \textit{links}, and \textit{satellites} so that structural change is additive rather than destructive. This thesis previews it here only to complete the comparative frame; Chapter~2 examines its mechanics and limits in depth.

\section{Modern Decision-Making Architectures}
\label{sec:ch1-modern-arch}

Modern architectures arose to absorb pressures the classical warehouse could not: data velocity, source heterogeneity, and demand for machine learning on the same data. Each relaxes a specific constraint. Read through the dual-scalability lens, they target the analytical and volume dimensions far more than the structural-modeling one.

\paragraph{Lambda architecture.}
Addresses the trade-off between latency and accuracy with two parallel pipelines \cite{marz2015} \textit{(industry practice; not peer-reviewed)}.

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{Batch layer} recomputes accurate views over an immutable master dataset.
  \item \textbf{Speed layer} serves low-latency approximate results over recent data.
  \item \textbf{Serving layer} merges the two.
  \item \textbf{Cost} the same business logic is implemented and maintained twice, once per layer.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Kappa architecture.}
Eliminates that duplication by treating all processing as stream processing over a replayable log \cite{kreps2014} \textit{(industry practice; not peer-reviewed)}. A single code path handles real-time and historical computation; history is reprocessed by replaying the log. Lambda and Kappa both relieve velocity pressure, but neither introduces a discipline for structural schema evolution \textemdash{} both assume the \textit{processing logic}, not the source schema, is what changes.

\paragraph{Data lake $\rightarrow$ Lakehouse.}
A shift toward storage flexibility and workload plurality.

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{Data lake} introduced schema-on-read over open file formats, accepting raw data of any shape \textemdash{} but lacked governance, transactional semantics, and BI-grade query performance.
  \item \textbf{Lakehouse} layers ACID table semantics and a metadata layer over open columnar formats such as Parquet and ORC \cite{armbrust2021}. Open table layers \textemdash{} Delta Lake \cite{deltalake2020}, Iceberg, Hudi \textemdash{} add transactions, time travel, and schema enforcement directly on the lake, so BI and ML run on one governed store. Peer-reviewed surveys confirm this as the consolidating direction of enterprise data architecture \cite{harby2024, schneider2024}.
  \item \textbf{Gap} the open table layer governs \textit{file} transactions and schema enforcement, not entity-level lineage or historization discipline \textemdash{} the structural-modeling gap remains.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Delta architecture (medallion).}
The dominant implementation pattern for the Lakehouse \cite{databricks2022} \textit{(industry practice; not peer-reviewed)}, organized as three zones:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{Bronze} raw ingested data.
  \item \textbf{Silver} cleansed and conformed data.
  \item \textbf{Gold} business-ready aggregates.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The pattern maps the classical ETL/ELT pipeline \cite{vassiliadis2009} onto governed table layers, so refinement is incremental and each zone is independently queryable.

\fig[0.98\textwidth]{fig_arch_evolution.png}{Data architecture evolution: from Classical DW to Lakehouse. Each arrow labels the constraint relaxed by the transition. The Medallion pattern is an implementation discipline within the Lakehouse, not a fourth peer architecture. \textit{Author-generated.}}{arch-evolution}

\noindent These architectures advance the analytical and volume frontiers, yet none supplies the modeling discipline that governs structural lineage and historization. Their value, and their gap, becomes precise only once the analytical demand they serve is specified \textemdash{} which the maturity spectrum does next.

\section{The Analytical Maturity Spectrum}
\label{sec:ch1-maturity}

Analytical capability progresses through four levels, and each level imposes a sharper architectural demand than the last \cite{gartner2012ascendancy} \textit{(industry practice; not peer-reviewed)}:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{Descriptive} \textemdash{} \textit{what happened?}
  \item \textbf{Diagnostic} \textemdash{} \textit{why did it happen?}
  \item \textbf{Predictive} \textemdash{} \textit{what will happen?}
  \item \textbf{Prescriptive} \textemdash{} \textit{what should be done?}
\end{itemize}

\noindent The requirements rise steeply across these steps. Descriptive and diagnostic analysis are served by the data warehouse and OLAP. Predictive analysis requires feature pipelines and a machine learning training capability grounded in statistical learning \cite{jordan2015}. Prescriptive analysis requires a closed feedback loop, model governance, and data fresh enough to act on; where decisions run over streaming data it also requires online learning over evolving distributions \cite{bifet2018}. The higher levels are not extensions of reporting \textemdash{} they demand a different pipeline shape. Table~\ref{tab:maturity-spectrum} maps each level to its requirement.

\noindent The jump from diagnostic to predictive is where the architecture, not just the query, must change. Yet the empirical reality is that most organizations remain at the descriptive and diagnostic levels: industry surveys report that the large majority have not reached transformational analytics maturity \cite{gartner2018maturity} \textit{(industry practice; not peer-reviewed)}. A peer-reviewed review of maturity models adds the relevant caution \textemdash{} the consulting models in common use lack theoretical grounding, leaving maturity defined without a rigorous instrument \cite{dataanalyticsmaturity2024}. That absence is part of what the next section repairs.

The implication is structural, not incremental. Reaching the prescriptive level requires the decision pipeline to be designed for native analytical capability from the outset, not extended with downstream additions. This is the demand that the \textit{analytical scalability} axis \textemdash{} one half of the dual construct defined next \textemdash{} is built to measure.

\section{Conceptual Framing of Scalability in DSIS}
\label{sec:ch1-scalability}

The survey reveals a consistent split: existing approaches advance either structural evolution or analytical integration, but never frame both as dimensions of one property, and never combine them into a single instrument. Data Vault~2.0 advances structural evolution; the Lakehouse advances analytical integration. Neither is articulated as a form of \textit{scalability}, and the two are never read together. This section closes that gap by formalizing both as the construct \textbf{C1}.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Definition (C1).} \textit{Scalability in DSIS} is the capacity of the system to grow \textemdash{} structurally and analytically \textemdash{} without re-engineering the existing architecture.
\end{quote}

\noindent The definition resolves into two distinct axes, each made assessable by five concrete dimensions:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{Structural scalability} how the system absorbs change in its data and sources.
  \item \textbf{Analytical scalability} how far the system can progress in analytical capability.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Structural Scalability}
\label{subsec:ch1-structural}

The capacity of a DSIS to evolve its data structure and integration footprint without disruptive re-engineering. Without it, every new source or schema change forces a partial rebuild. Five dimensions characterize it:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{Source integration} the cost of absorbing a new heterogeneous data source, measured in effort, schema impact, and pipeline disruption.
  \item \textbf{Schema evolution} the cost and blast radius of structural changes (column renames, entity splits, type changes), and the degree to which they are non-destructive and version-aware.
  \item \textbf{Volume and throughput growth} the capacity to scale data volume and ingestion load without architectural redesign.
  \item \textbf{Historization and auditability} the completeness and non-destructiveness of the historical record, and the ability to audit changes over time.
  \item \textbf{Loosely-coupled change propagation} the degree to which a structural change in one source or layer is isolated from downstream consumers.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Analytical Scalability}
\label{subsec:ch1-analytical}

The capacity of a DSIS to progress along the maturity spectrum \textemdash{} descriptive toward prescriptive \textemdash{} with advanced capability integrated \textit{natively} in the decision pipeline rather than bolted on downstream. Without it, predictive and prescriptive capability can only be grafted externally, outside the governed pipeline. Five dimensions characterize it:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{Maturity reach} the highest maturity level the architecture supports natively, from descriptive through prescriptive.
  \item \textbf{Native model integration} the degree to which ML and predictive models are first-class citizens of the pipeline rather than external appendages.
  \item \textbf{Data readiness for analytics} the freshness, trust, and feature availability of data at the point of analytical consumption.
  \item \textbf{Workload plurality} the capacity to serve BI and ML workloads from the same governed data layer without duplication.
  \item \textbf{Lifecycle support} the support for model retraining, feedback loops, and model governance within the pipeline.
\end{itemize}

\noindent \textbf{The two axes are orthogonal.} A system can be:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{Structurally scalable yet analytically capped} \textemdash{} as classical Data Vault~2.0 is, absorbing schema change yet reaching no further than descriptive analysis.
  \item \textbf{Analytically capable yet structurally undisciplined} \textemdash{} as a raw data lake is, supporting ML yet governing neither lineage nor historization.
\end{itemize}

\noindent A coherent scalable DSIS must therefore address both axes jointly, not trade one for the other. The next section applies this lens to every surveyed approach and shows that none does.

\section{Synthesis: Critical Reading and the Research Gap}
\label{sec:ch1-gap}

Read through the dual-scalability lens, no surveyed approach scores well on both axes at once. Each is strong on a subset of dimensions and weak on the rest, and that pattern of strengths and weaknesses \textit{is} the research gap. The four candidate approaches assess as follows:

\paragraph{Classical paradigms (Inmon, Kimball).}
Moderate structurally, capped analytically. Volume growth is manageable, but schema evolution is poor, source integration is rigid, historization is limited to anticipated SCD types, and change propagation is tightly coupled through the normalized core or conformed dimensions. Analytically they stop at descriptive and diagnostic and cannot progress without an external bolt-on.

\paragraph{Data lake.}
Inverts that profile on the structural side. It scales volume and absorbs heterogeneous sources easily, but imposes no modeling discipline \textemdash{} schema-evolution governance, historization, and change-propagation isolation are all weak. Its analytical ceiling is potentially high, yet poor data quality and absent governance undermine data readiness, so the potential is rarely realized.

\paragraph{Lakehouse.}
Strong analytically, partial structurally. ACID semantics, unified BI and ML workloads, and open formats give it strength across every analytical dimension. Structurally it handles volume and schema-on-read well, but it provides no modeling discipline for structural lineage and historization equivalent to Data Vault~2.0.

\paragraph{Data Vault~2.0.}
Strong structurally, limited analytically \textemdash{} the mirror image of the Lakehouse. It is designed for schema evolution, source integration, historization, and change propagation, but does not natively support ML workloads or progression along the maturity spectrum. Chapter~2 examines this profile in depth.

Their combination is therefore the most promising candidate for a jointly scalable DSIS, yet that composability is asserted in industry practice and under-examined conceptually. That examination is the task of Chapter~2.

\section*{Conclusion}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Conclusion}

This chapter delivered two things:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{A grounded survey} \textemdash{} the vocabulary of DSIS, from the classical warehouse paradigms through the modern Lakehouse and the analytical maturity spectrum.
  \item \textbf{The construct C1} (\S\ref{sec:ch1-scalability}) \textemdash{} structural and analytical scalability as two orthogonal axes, each operationalized by five assessable dimensions: the single lens the literature had been missing.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Applying C1 in \S\ref{sec:ch1-gap} exposed the gap. No single existing approach is strong on both axes, and the near-complementary profiles of Data Vault~2.0 and the Lakehouse make their combination the leading candidate \textemdash{} with their composability still unexamined. Chapter~2 subjects Data Vault~2.0 and the Lakehouse to a systematic critical analysis along these dimensions, developing the composability examination (C2).
