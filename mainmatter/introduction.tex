\uchapter{General Introduction}

\section*{Context}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Context}
\label{sec:intro-context}

Organizations make decisions on the evidence their information systems can supply. For three decades that evidence has been assembled in the data warehouse, the integrated and query-oriented store at the core of decision support \cite{inmon2005, kimball2013}. The warehouse was engineered for a stable world: a fixed set of relational sources, a schema settled at design time, and an analytical scope limited to reporting on what already happened \cite{golfarelli2009}. That world no longer holds.

Two changes have outrun the original design. The first is in the data itself. The number and variety of sources feeding the decision layer have grown sharply, and transactional systems, streaming feeds, change-data-capture pipelines, and semi-structured data now arrive together \cite{vassiliadis2009, deltalake2020}. A schema fixed before ingestion becomes a liability the moment a new source or a business change invalidates it \cite{kleppmann2017}. The second change is in what organizations ask of their data. Analytical ambition has moved along the descriptive, diagnostic, predictive, and prescriptive spectrum \cite{davenport2007}. Most organizations still operate at the descriptive and diagnostic levels, yet demand for predictive and prescriptive capability grows steadily \cite{chen2012}. The decision layer is now expected to forecast and recommend, not only to report.

These two changes name two distinct concerns. The first is \textit{structural scalability}: the capacity of a warehouse architecture to absorb new sources, schema changes, and volume growth without disruptive re-engineering. The second is \textit{analytical scalability}: the capacity of an architecture to support progressively more advanced analytics, from business intelligence through prediction, on the same data. Both are properties of the architecture, and both have been studied extensively, but largely apart.

\section*{Problem and Motivation}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Problem and Motivation}
\label{sec:intro-problem}

The literature on decision support architectures addresses these two concerns in separate conversations. Structural evolution is studied through warehouse modeling: the classical paradigms of Inmon and Kimball, and the later Data Vault~2.0 discipline built specifically to absorb schema change \cite{inmon2005, kimball2013, linstedt2015}. Analytical capability is studied through modern data platforms: the Lambda and Kappa streaming designs, the data lake, and the Lakehouse paradigm that unifies business intelligence and machine learning on open storage \cite{marz2015, kreps2014, armbrust2021}. Each body of work is mature on its own. What is missing is a consolidated reading of the field that compares architectures on \textit{both} concerns and asks what already exists at their intersection.

This thesis takes that gap as its purpose. It does not propose a new architecture. It surveys the existing approaches on each axis, compares them against criteria drawn from the literature, and reports what the literature itself concludes about which approaches best serve each form of scalability and about the architectures that combine the two.

\section*{Objectives}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Objectives}
\label{sec:intro-objectives}

The thesis pursues three objectives, each a survey-and-comparison task rather than a design task.

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=4pt]
  \item Survey the main data-warehouse modeling approaches and compare them on structural scalability, concluding which the literature finds best suited to absorb source and schema change.
  \item Survey the main modern analytical architectures and compare them on analytical scalability, concluding which the literature finds best suited to support the full analytical spectrum.
  \item Review what the literature already reports on architectures and approaches that address both forms of scalability together, and characterize the current state of that intersection.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Every conclusion drawn in the thesis traces to a cited source. Where evidence rests on industry or vendor publications rather than peer-reviewed work, the text flags it explicitly and weighs it accordingly.

\section*{Methodology}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Methodology}
\label{sec:intro-methodology}

The thesis is a theoretical state-of-the-art study; it builds no prototype and runs no experiment. Its method is a narrative, semi-systematic literature review. The review draws on three tiers of source. The foundational tier comprises the standard texts on warehouse modeling and design \cite{inmon2005, kimball2013, linstedt2015, golfarelli2009}. The current tier comprises peer-reviewed work from 2020--2026 on Lakehouse architectures, streaming analytics, and machine-learning operations \cite{armbrust2021, schneider2024, harby2024, nambiar2022}. A third tier comprises authoritative industry and vendor sources that document current practice ahead of the academic record; these are used only to report what practitioners do, are flagged in-text as industry sources, and are never treated as peer-reviewed evidence. Comparison proceeds against explicit criteria, defined in each chapter from the surveyed literature, so that the conclusions read as assessments rather than opinion.

\section*{Thesis Outline}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Thesis Outline}
\label{sec:intro-outline}

The body has three chapters. Chapter~1 surveys data-warehouse modeling approaches --- Inmon, Kimball, Data Vault~2.0, and modern variants --- and compares them on structural scalability. Chapter~2 surveys modern analytical architectures --- Lambda, Kappa, the data lake, the Lakehouse, and the Delta architecture --- and compares them on analytical scalability. Chapter~3 reviews the smaller body of work on architectures that address both forms of scalability at once, centered on the documented combination of Data Vault~2.0 with the Lakehouse. The General Conclusion consolidates the findings, states the limits of a literature-based study, and identifies the directions the literature points toward.
